# 40 Hz, Take Two

> [!WARNING]
> This flickers 40 times a second. Skip the light if you have epilepsy, photosensitivity or migraines. Use at your own risk.

Light that flickers and sound that pulses 40 times a second is one of the odder ideas in Alzheimer's research. It came out of Li-Huei Tsai's lab at MIT. In mice it cut amyloid and helped memory. In people it's safe so far and still unproven, and Cognito's 670-person HOPE trial should report this year. The research, sources and all, is in [findings.md](https://github.com/heathdutton/40hz/blob/main/findings.md).

It's also cheap. A good screen and headphones can deliver it... if they're set up right. That "if" is why this is my second attempt.

## Take one

The first version was a simpler web page. It never quite worked, and it took me a while to see why. It was arithmetic.

A screen can't flicker faster than half its refresh rate, and a clean 40 Hz needs a refresh that divides evenly by 40. At 120 Hz each cycle is 3 frames, 1 on and 2 off. At 60 Hz, which is most laptops and monitors, 40 Hz comes out as a lopsided 20 Hz. 144 and 165 can't do it either.

Then the software piles on:

- Safari holds pages to 60 fps on a 120 Hz screen, and to 30 in Low Power Mode.
- macOS can report 120 Hz in Low Power Mode while the panel actually runs at 60.
- YouTube tops out at 60 fps, so a "40 Hz" video can't flicker at 40 Hz on any screen.

So most devices that ran my first page were showing something else, and nothing said so.

I also damaged a Retina display along the way. I wasn't careful enough.

## Test first

This time the page measures before it shows anything. It times 2.5 seconds of frames, snaps the result to a real panel rate (119.88 reads as 120), and only offers the light if that rate divides evenly by 40. On a 60 Hz laptop the light stays off and the page says why. If it's Safari holding a 120 Hz screen back, the page walks you through the setting, since only you can change it.

Sound has no such limit. Audio timing is exact on any device, so sound runs everywhere.

The light runs on more hardware than last time, too. Without WebGPU or HDR it still works, just no brighter than normal white. There's also a prerendered video path, a tiny HDR video with the flicker baked in. The compositor plays it, so a busy page can't make it stutter.

## Proving it

When the light runs, the session counts what actually happened. Dropped frames and the longest gap go in the stats, and every session ends with a plain verdict, from "Clean" to "Not usable". On the video path it counts frames the compositor presented, the only honest counter there.

The light follows the sound's clock. Audio and display clocks drift ~180 ms an hour against each other, about 7 full cycles. Slaving the light to the audio costs a one-frame correction about every three minutes, and those get counted too.

The browser still only knows what it sent, not what the panel showed. The one real check is a light sensor (a ~$1 photodiode into a mic input shows the actual flicker).

## Safer

- A warning before any light, and no autoplay. Sound only is always one click away.
- Click anywhere or hit Esc to stop. Sessions also end on a timer, even in a background tab.
- Never saturated red.
- HDR defaults to 2x normal white. The web hides a panel's real headroom, so the page assumes a conservative 3x.
- A gentler green/blue shimmer, a softer dark level and a smaller field are all in settings.

## The sound

Noise weighted toward 250 to 500 Hz, pulsed 40 times a second with a sharp start and a quick fade. The 40 Hz response is ~3x bigger on a 250 Hz carrier than on 4 kHz, and older ears lose the highs first. Same pulse in both ears, no binaural beats. You can load your own music and it gets the same pulse.

## What it won't do

It doesn't treat anything, and it doesn't claim to. Cognito's earlier trial missed its main goal. This is a way to experiment with the stimulus, not a therapy.

On a 60 Hz screen you get sound only. And without a light sensor, "Clean" means the browser thinks it hit 40 Hz.
